# Sensing and seeing: aither between a sensor and a display

**Status: brainstorm.** Nothing here is built or decided. These are use
cases to keep in mind while the language settles, plus the questions
they raise for it.

## The idea

The pair output already says the sink gives the numbers their meaning.
The same goes for the source. `input()` reads a pair stream from stdin,
and `AITHER_RATE` sets the rate. So a sound card with a coil, a whip
antenna or a Geiger counter on its line input turns aither into the
detector:

```
arecord -f FLOAT_LE -c 2 -r 96000 | AITHER_RATE=96000 aither vlf.aither | aplay …   # hear it
arecord … | aither loops.aither | scope                                             # see it
```

The patch is the measuring instrument. You can edit it live while the
signal keeps coming in, and all the filter state survives. That is
where aither differs from a fixed meter. You find a hum and write a
notch for it. A sferic turns up and you add a trigger. Each of those
is one line, sent while you listen.

Sections 1–3 are sensing, ordered by how easy each signal is to get. Sections 4–6 add RF and 5G metering, electrical work, and what happens when the same patch both makes a signal and measures it. Section 7 covers the pictures.

## 1. EMF reader (TriField TF2-style)

The TF2 shows three readings: the AC magnetic field (about 40 Hz to
100 kHz, in milligauss), the AC electric field (V/m) and RF (20 MHz to
6 GHz). The first two fit a sound card. The third doesn't.

**Magnetic.** A pickup coil goes into the line input. A telephone
pickup coil works, or an inductor, or a few hundred turns on a ferrite
rod. The coil outputs dB/dt, not B, so the patch integrates it with a
leaky integrator (1/f) to get a flat field reading. To calibrate, use a
long straight wire carrying a known current: B = μ₀I / 2πr. After that
the patch can display real milligauss.

**Electric.** A short wire antenna feeds a high-impedance JFET buffer.
This is the same front end the VLF receiver below uses.

**What to build in the patch:**
- A meter. RMS over a window, shown as a number or as a bar on `scope`.
- Mains analysis: the level of 50/60 Hz and each harmonic separately.
  A dimmer, an SMPS or a motor each leaves its own fingerprint in the
  harmonic ratios.
- A sniffer, which is the part the TF2 can't do: *listen* to the field.
  Shift the content above 20 kHz down by heterodyning, and every
  charger, LED driver and CPU gets its own voice. Christina Kubisch's
  *Electrical Walks* (induction headphones around a city) is the art
  precedent for this.
- A hazard to know about: the uConsole is itself a loud emitter, with
  the CPU, the display and the charging circuit. A short cable, or
  noting where the device's own lines sit, is part of the setup. It is
  also a good first patch: *what does the uConsole sound like?*

**RF** (MHz to GHz) needs a log detector chip (for example AD8317) whose
output *voltage* can then go into the sound card as a slow envelope. An
SDR is also an option (see 3).

## 2. Natural radio: VLF and lightning

VLF (roughly 0.3 to 30 kHz) is already in the audio range. A whip
antenna and preamp (DIY JFET, or a kit such as the BBB-4 or VLF-3)
plugs into a sound card. Use `AITHER_RATE=96000` so that 24 kHz and
above aren't lost. You have to get away from mains wiring, ideally by
kilometres, and listen at night or at dawn.

**What you hear:**
| Signal | Sound | Source |
|---|---|---|
| sferics | clicks and crackle | lightning, up to thousands of km away |
| tweeks | short pinging chirp, ~1.7 kHz cutoff | sferics dispersed in the Earth–ionosphere waveguide, mostly at night |
| whistlers | falling tone, 1 to 10 kHz, seconds long | lightning energy that has travelled along the Earth's magnetic field to the other hemisphere and back |
| dawn chorus | rising chirps, like birds | electrons in the magnetosphere, mostly around dawn and in storms |
| hiss | band of noise | magnetosphere |

**What aither is good for here:**
- **Hum removal.** A comb of notches at 50/60 Hz and its harmonics,
  tuned live until the hum is gone. This is the single most useful VLF
  patch, and live tuning suits it well.
- **Sferic detector.** Impulse detection, then a strike rate per
  minute. That gives a rough "is a storm coming" indicator, and the
  rate trend matters more than any single count.
- **Direction finding with stereo.** Two crossed loop antennas go in,
  N–S on L and E–W on R. On an x-y display each sferic draws a line
  pointing at the storm. This is the classic crossed-loop goniometer,
  and it is *exactly* the pair-as-x,y idea from SCOPE.md, run with a
  receiver instead of a synth. There is a 180° ambiguity. Resolving it
  needs a third channel (an E-field sense antenna), which means a
  sound card with more than two inputs.
- **Range from tweeks.** At night a tweek's dispersion near the
  waveguide cutoff gives a distance estimate from a single station.
- **Whistler dechirp.** A whistler follows t ≈ D/√f. A patch that
  undoes that dispersion collapses the whistler back into a click and
  estimates D. That is a measurement of the path through the
  magnetosphere.
- **Sudden ionospheric disturbances (solar flares).** The same receiver
  picks up military VLF transmitters such as NAA 24.0 kHz, NWC 19.8 kHz,
  DHO38 23.4 kHz and GQD 22.1 kHz. A flare ionises the D layer, and
  their signal strength jumps within minutes. Log the carrier level
  over a day and you have a solar flare monitor. This needs 96 kHz.
- **Schumann resonances** (7.83 Hz and harmonics): the hardest one.
  Most sound cards block DC and roll off below a few Hz to 20 Hz, so it
  needs a DC-coupled ADC and a big induction coil. A stretch goal.

The networked version of lightning location is Blitzortung (crossed
loops plus E-field, GPS time of arrival across many stations). That is
a reference point, not a target.

## 3. "Interstellar radiation": what actually reaches a backpack

What reaches the ground from beyond the solar system is mostly
**muons**, from cosmic-ray air showers, about one per cm² per minute at
sea level. There is also a **radio** sky. The realistic options:

- **Geiger counter clicks.** Most counters have an audio or pulse
  output. Feed it into `input()`, count the pulses, show CPM and the
  dose rate. Simple, and it works now.
- **Muon coincidence with stereo.** Stack two GM tubes (or two
  scintillator paddles, e.g. CosmicWatch), one on L and one on R. A
  pulse on *both* channels within a few samples is most likely a muon
  passing through both. Local radioactivity rarely hits both at once.
  At 48 kHz a sample is about 21 µs and GM pulses are about 100 µs
  wide, so a coincidence window of one to a few samples is reasonable.
  Tilt the stack and the rate follows cos² of the zenith angle. That
  is a real cosmic-ray experiment on a uConsole.
- **Radio astronomy via an SDR** (the uConsole's AIO board has an
  RTL-SDR, if fitted):
  - **The hydrogen line at 1420 MHz**: emission from neutral hydrogen
    *in interstellar space*, with a small dish or horn. This is the
    most literal "interstellar" signal an amateur can detect. The SDR
    does the RF. aither could take the downconverted IQ as its pair
    input (I on L, Q on R) and do the integration and display.
  - **Jupiter decametric bursts** around 20 MHz (NASA's Radio JOVE
    project): the receiver's audio output is the signal, and it
    sounds like surf.
  - **The Sun** in the same bands, and solar radio bursts.

IQ in as a pair is worth noting. The pair-output note already sends an
aither pair *to* an SDR, so the reverse direction is symmetrical.

## 4. RF and 5G meter (for people who are EMF-sensitive)

A sound card can't sample GHz, but it can sample the *envelope*. A log
detector chip (AD8317: 1 MHz to 10 GHz, output in volts proportional to
dBm) turns RF power into a slow voltage, and that voltage goes into the
line input. This covers the cellular bands below 6 GHz, including 5G
n78 at 3.5 GHz, plus Wi-Fi, DECT and Bluetooth. 5G mmWave (24 to
47 GHz) needs a specialist detector. It is also rare outside dense
city centres and is stopped by walls.

**Telling sources apart by rhythm.** Each technology pulses at its own
rate, and the envelope carries that rate:

| Source | Rhythm in the envelope |
|---|---|
| Wi-Fi beacon | ~10 Hz (102.4 ms beacon interval) |
| DECT cordless phone | 100 Hz (10 ms frame) |
| GSM | 217 Hz |
| 5G NR (TDD) | 10 ms frames; 0.5 ms slots at 30 kHz spacing (a 2 kHz buzz); sync bursts every 20 ms by default |

Commercial RF meters have an audio mode that plays this envelope, which
is how experienced users identify sources by ear. aither can play the
same buzz *and* name the source: a small bank of resonators on the
envelope, one per rhythm, each with its own level meter.

**What the meter should show:**
- Power density in µW/m² or V/m, calibrated against a reference source.
- Both reference scales, labelled: the ICNIRP public exposure limits
  (watts per m² in these bands) and the much stricter
  building-biology guidance values (SBM-2015, µW/m²) that many
  EMF-sensitive people use. Showing the number and where it falls on
  each scale lets the user decide, without the meter picking for them.
- A log over time, so "it's worse in the evening" becomes a graph.

A note on sensitivity: in blinded provocation studies, people who
report electromagnetic hypersensitivity haven't detected fields better
than chance. The symptoms are real; the cause is disputed. A useful
honest feature: a logging mode where the user marks moments they feel
symptoms, which can then be checked against what the meter actually
recorded at those times. That is a self-test anyone can run, whichever
way it comes out.

With an SDR the meter can also show *which* band and operator each
signal is. An RTL-SDR reaches about 1.7 GHz, which covers the
600–900 MHz cellular bands. n78 at 3.5 GHz needs something like a
HackRF (up to 6 GHz).

## 5. Electrical work

A sound card makes a usable two-channel oscilloscope and signal
generator for anything below about 20 kHz. aither then supplies the
measuring logic.

**Safety first:** never connect mains to a sound card. Line inputs take
about 1 to 2 V. Use a coil, a current clamp, or a small isolation
transformer with a divider.

- **Live wire finder.** The E-field front end from section 1 shows
  50/60 Hz where a live cable runs behind plaster. A field that stops
  partway along a run points to a break.
- **Cable tracer with lock-in detection.** aither sends a tone down a
  *dead* cable (headphone out, through a series capacitor) and listens
  with an induction probe. Because the patch made the tone, it can
  multiply what comes back by the same tone and average the result.
  That is a lock-in amplifier, and it picks the tone out from far below
  the hum. A commercial toner just listens. This one measures.
- **Mains quality.** Grid frequency (it drifts by about ±0.05 Hz with
  load), THD, and the harmonics a given appliance adds. Grid-frequency
  drift is also how forensic analysts date recordings (ENF analysis),
  which would make a neat side project.
- **Frequency response analyser.** Sweep a sine out, read it back, plot
  gain and phase. For filters, speakers, crossovers and guitar pedals.
  Same lock-in trick.
- **Metal detector.** An induction-balance detector works at 3 to
  30 kHz, which is audio range. One coil transmits aither's tone, a
  second overlapping coil is nulled, and metal shifts the phase of
  what returns. The pitch of the output can encode the phase, so iron
  and copper sound different.
- **Machine listening.** A coil near a motor, or a contact mic on a
  bearing. Bearing faults show up as specific harmonics of the shaft
  speed.

## 6. Synthesis with the world in it

The core of aither is still sound. Sensors give it new inputs:

- **Sonification as music.** Strike rate drives the tempo, the field
  level drives the filter, muon coincidences trigger notes. A piece
  that is different every night because the sky is.
- **Natural radio as a source.** Whistlers and chorus as raw material,
  live-processed: pitch-shifted, granulated, fed into a resonator.
- **Active sensing is synthesis.** The cable tracer, the sweep analyser
  and the metal detector are all patches that make a sound and listen
  to how the world changes it. That is the cybernetic loop in
  [cybernetics.md](cybernetics.md), with the room or the ground as
  part of the circuit.

## 7. Pictures: math displays, generative art and video filters

[displays-2026-10-16.md](displays-2026-10-16.md) already decides the
displays (`scope` for vectors, `tv` for raster with domain colouring)
and covers fractals, MilkDrop-style feedback and reaction-diffusion.
That note covers this ground. What's new here is mostly input.

**Math displays.** Domain colouring *is* a complex-function plotter:
make the pixel's value f(z) and tv draws the function, zeros as black
points with the colour wheel turning round them. Phase portraits of
ODEs on `scope`. Lissajous figures and harmonographs as teaching
pieces. Sensor data on the same displays: crossed-loop bearings (2), a
whistler waterfall (2), an RF log (4).

**Generative art.** Already the visuals engine's job. Sensing adds
generative art driven by the environment: a picture that grows with
each lightning strike or each muon.

**Video filters.** A camera can be another source of pairs:

```
cam --size 64x48 | aither vfx.aither | tv --size 64x48
```

- `cam` (not written) would read frames from the webcam through
  ffmpeg or v4l2 and write one pair per pixel in raster order. Encode
  colour as chroma: Cb on one channel and Cr on the other, scaled by
  luma. tv's domain colouring reads phase as hue and magnitude as
  brightness, so a pixel comes back out looking roughly like its
  original colour. No filter in between means you see the camera.
- The coincidence that makes this practical: **64×48 at 15.6 fps is
  48 000 pixels a second, the audio rate.** Video at that size *is* an
  audio-rate stream. The same engine and pipe work unchanged.
- Audio filters applied to video do what the analogue video
  synthesisers did (Sandin Image Processor, Rutt/Etra): a lowpass
  smears along each line, a resonant filter rings at edges, a delay of
  w samples looks one row up. A state array of w×h gives frame
  feedback, trails, slit-scan and time displacement.
- The camera on one channel and the music on the other is not
  possible. Video uses both channels. The music-reactive version
  needs the input-rate option from the displays note, or a second
  input.
- ffmpeg isn't on the uConsole yet.

## What these ask of the language (open questions)

These are not proposals. They are pressure points to discuss.

1. **Slow outputs.** A detector produces "12 strikes/min" or "34 CPM",
   not audio. Today the only output is the pair stream. Options:
   encode readings in the pair and let a small sink tool decode them,
   the way `scope` reads the pair as x,y; or add a side channel for
   numbers. The pair-only design suggests the first.
2. **Events and counting.** Threshold, hold-off and counting over a
   window. These are probably stdlib defs on `$state`, not language
   features. Worth checking that they're easy to write.
3. **More than two input channels.** Crossed loops plus an E-field
   antenna needs three. Is the pair a hard limit for input as well as
   output?
4. **Time.** Logging SIDs across a day, or comparing strikes with
   another station, needs wall-clock or GPS time. `t` starts at 0.
5. **Rate.** VLF wants 96k, IQ from an SDR wants whatever the SDR
   gives. `AITHER_RATE` already covers this. Check that the filters
   still behave at 96k and 192k (they're written in Hz, so they should).
6. **Spectral views.** Whistlers and chorus are best seen on a
   spectrogram (a waterfall), which suits `tv` (the raster display),
   not `scope`. Is a sliding FFT something a patch does, or something
   the display does?
7. **CPU budget.** The uConsole runs with its fast cores off. Comb
   notches and integrators are cheap. FFTs and dechirping may belong
   in offline `audit`-style passes on recordings.
8. **Output and input on one clock.** A lock-in amplifier, a sweep
   analyser or a metal detector sends a signal out and reads it back.
   With `arecord | aither | aplay` those are two separate devices with
   an unknown delay between them. I/Q detection tolerates a fixed
   phase offset, but a delay that moves silently is not fine. Answered
   by `card` ([card-2026-10-16.md](card-2026-10-16.md)).
9. **A camera as a source.** Video in needs a small `cam` program, the
   reverse of `tv`: frames in, pairs out. Like the displays, it would
   live in `tools/`. Not a language change.

## A first step that needs nothing new

Take a coil (or even a bare wire), plug it into the line input, then
run `arecord … | aither emf.aither | aplay …` with a patch that
heterodynes everything above 15 kHz down into hearing range, plus an
RMS meter. Walk it round the house. If that sounds interesting, the
rest is antennas.
